# Image Resonance Principle

Image Fidelity is not determined solely
by object accuracy.

An image may correctly depict:

- people
- places
- objects
- actions

and still feel wrong.

The participant's response
is part of image evaluation.

## Core Question

Ask:

"Does the participant recognize
their testimony in the image?"

This question precedes
technical image evaluation.

## Object Fidelity

Object Fidelity asks:

- Are the visible elements correct?
- Are the objects present?
- Is the scene technically accurate?

Object Fidelity is important.

Object Fidelity is not sufficient.

## Character Fidelity

Character Fidelity asks:

- Does the person feel like the person in the testimony?
- Does the atmosphere of the person match the testimony?
- Does the posture, expression, manner, and presence fit?
- Does the cultural and regional context shape the person appropriately?

A person is not defined only by:

- age
- gender
- clothing
- relationship

A person also carries:

- atmosphere
- manner
- dignity
- temperament
- cultural context
- regional feeling

## Relationship Does Not Determine Appearance

Family roles do not determine visual appearance.

Grandmother does not mean elderly.

Grandfather does not mean old.

Mother does not mean middle-aged.

Child does not mean young-looking.

Relationship describes who someone is.

Testimony describes how they appeared.

Testimony takes priority.

## Cultural and Regional Atmosphere

Regional context may shape
the atmosphere of a person.

For example:

- Kyoto may suggest refinement, calmness, restraint, quiet dignity.
- Tokyo Fukagawa may suggest Edo-style clarity, briskness, directness.
- Osaka may suggest warmth, liveliness, expressive familiarity.
- Tokyo Katsushika may suggest downtown simplicity and approachability.

These are not rigid stereotypes.

They are contextual atmospheres.

Use them only when they support
the testimony.

Do not reduce people
to caricatures.

Do not exaggerate regional traits.

Preserve dignity.

## Witness Fidelity

Witness Fidelity asks:

- Does the image preserve testimony?
- Does the participant feel represented?
- Does the image resonate with what remained?

An image may have high Object Fidelity
and low Witness Fidelity.

An image may depict correct objects
and still fail the testimony.

## Participant Response

Participant reactions are valid evaluation signals.

Examples:

- "That's it."
- "Close."
- "Something feels wrong."
- "That's not them."
- "That feels strange."
- "That feels unpleasant."

These responses should not be dismissed.

They provide information
about image resonance.

## Resonance Before Validation

Before asking:

"Is the image correct?"

ask:

"Does the image resonate?"

A technically correct image
may fail resonance.

An imperfect image
may resonate deeply.

## Pattern Recognition

When an image feels wrong,
do not immediately modify objects.

First observe:

- what feels wrong
- what feels missing
- what feels unfamiliar
- what feels unlike the testimony
- what atmosphere is wrong
- what character quality is missing

Make the pattern visible.

Do not force interpretation.

## Fidelity Over Stereotype

If there is a conflict between:

- testimony
- cultural expectation
- social stereotype
- visual cliché

testimony always wins.

The system must preserve
what was witnessed,
not what is statistically expected.

## Image Fidelity Check

Before validating an image, ask:

1. Are the objects correct?

2. Is the person visually faithful?

3. Does the character atmosphere fit?

4. Is any stereotype overriding testimony?

5. Does the participant recognize
   their testimony in the image?

If not,

the image fails.

## One-Line Principle

Image fidelity is not only
what is shown.

It is whether the testimony
is recognizable in what is shown.
